## 3. What is OSINT?

OSINT means finding things out from public sources instead of secret ones. The question in the middle could lead us to photos, maps, news, public records or archives; the difficulty is deciding which sources help and whether they support the answer. It includes offline public material too, though I’m focusing on the web. The Bellingcat toolkit linked below collects tools practitioners use.

Sources: Bellingcat Online Investigation Toolkit — https://bellingcat.gitbook.io/toolkit

## 8. Those were two kinds of task

Those were two kinds of task, and there are many more. Alongside identifying objects and locating photos or videos, investigators work out when footage was filmed and check whether it supports its claimed context. They also follow accounts, domains or companies across sites, and reconstruct movements or events. The toolkit and framework links collect resources, while the Berkeley Protocol describes the broader investigation process.

Sources: Bellingcat Toolkit — https://bellingcat.gitbook.io/toolkit; OSINT Framework — https://osintframework.com/; Berkeley Protocol · investigation process — https://humanrights.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Berkeley-Protocol.pdf

## 9. Where it is used

Storyful verifies footage against maps and street-level views. Amnesty’s Evidence Lab supports human rights investigations through digital verification and remote sensing. Bellingcat reconstructed movements in the MH17 investigation from public photos and video. These are practitioner applications, and they’re why I think it’s worth exploring where an agent could contribute.

Sources: Storyful geolocation — https://storyful.com/news/verified-by-storyful/inside-storyful-verification-workflow-geolocation/; Amnesty Evidence Lab — https://citizenevidence.org/; Bellingcat MH17 report — https://www.bellingcat.com/news/europe/2015/10/08/mh17-the-open-source-evidence/

## 15. More searching helped, in a small pilot

Across five tasks and twenty-two digests, I ran fifteen executions. At cap one, four out of five outputs were valid, and twenty-three of thirty-one fields matched. At cap four, that became five out of five and thirty-one of thirty-one, and cap twelve gave the same results. More searching helped in this small setup, but these numbers measure digest agreement; we haven’t evaluated live-web performance or compared against a baseline agent.

Sources: Pilot results — pilot-results.txt

## 16. How we could run the study

I’d start by choosing a small subset of tasks and curating difficult cases that need several steps across sources, with checked answers and some cases held back from development. Then we’d give agents web access and record their evidence, dead ends and cost. Finally, we’d compare your loop with other agents, including a single-pass baseline, under the same tools, models and budgets. That’s the first study I’d like to work through together.
